\section{Interpretation and Error Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\paragraph{Which model was better.} On HH-eval, DPO-HH was better than SFT: it won 0.596 of the decided pairs, and every sensitivity check kept it between 0.580 and 0.613 (Table~\ref{tab:dpo}). On AlpacaEval, SFT was better than DPO-UF under default decoding: DPO-UF won only 0.392 of the decided pairs, so SFT won 0.608, but the two tie (0.498) when I apply a repetition penalty of 1.1 to both. On the other track's prompts, DPO-HH matched SFT on AlpacaEval (0.493), and DPO-UF was worse than SFT on HH-eval (0.417). On the persona prompts, the selected adapter was no better than the base model, and the adapter with the strongest style was worse on usefulness and safety (Table~\ref{tab:lorares}). Below, \emph{Measured} marks what the runs show and \emph{Interpretation} marks what I infer from it.

\paragraph{HH-RLHF DPO.} \emph{Measured.} The gain concentrates where DPO-HH answered more briefly. It won 0.725 of the decided pairs when its answer was under 0.8 times SFT's length (133 pairs) and 0.362 when it was over 1.2 times as long (52 pairs), and the 115 similar-length pairs were a statistical tie (0.554, $p=0.42$). Its answers were 20\% shorter (mean 190 against 237 tokens), more of them ended on their own (268 against 255 of 300), and fewer contained a repeated phrase (133 against 150). The advantage survives a repetition penalty that removes most loops (0.581), and no length group stands out there (0.55 to 0.61). Across prompt types (Table~\ref{tab:categories}), DPO-HH is ahead in nine of ten categories, but only hate or harassment (0.81) and calibration and uncertainty (0.73) have intervals that exclude 0.5, with 30 prompts per category, and self-harm support is behind (0.47). Refusal phrases barely changed overall (0.04 to 0.05) and doubled only for self-harm support (0.20 to 0.40).

\emph{Interpretation.} I read the HH result as a modest gain in staying brief and on topic, not a refusal effect. The refusal rate did not rise, the update was small (reward accuracy plateaued at 0.64 and the margin reached only 0.25 after one epoch at $\beta=0.1$), and part of the default-decoding gain comes from fewer loops, because the length effect shrinks once the penalty removes them. The accuracy plateau fits noisy preference labels, but I did not measure label noise. The examples in Table~\ref{tab:examples} show that both models often open with the same disclaimer or miss the task.

\paragraph{UltraFeedback DPO.} \emph{Measured.} The loss concentrates in pairs where DPO-UF wrote much more. It won 0.290 of the decided pairs when it was over 1.2 times as long as SFT (208 of 300 pairs) but 0.648 when the lengths were similar (81 pairs, $p=0.04$). Its answers were 49\% longer (mean 299 against 201 tokens), hit the 512-token cap more often (71 against 44), ended on their own less often (229 against 256 of 300), and contained a repeated phrase more often (177 against 123). The rubric's length guidance did not cause the loss, because dropping it gave 0.413. The repetition penalty did change it: repeated-phrase answers fell to 63 for DPO-UF and 33 for SFT, and the win rate rose to 0.498. The deficit appears in all five AlpacaEval sources (0.30 to 0.46, lowest on \texttt{helpful\_base}). On HH-eval, DPO-UF is behind in calibration and uncertainty (0.21), harmful instructions (0.25), and legal and financial safety (0.28), and it used a refusal phrase on 20\% of benign prompts against 3\% for SFT.

\emph{Interpretation.} The model learned UltraFeedback well (validation accuracy 0.71, margin 0.63), yet its answers got worse under greedy decoding, so preference accuracy did not track answer quality. Both implicit rewards fell (chosen $-0.12$, rejected $-0.75$), so the update pushed probability away from what SFT typically writes. The answers grew from SFT's 201 tokens to 299, although the training set's chosen responses average only 171 tokens and were only 15\% longer than the rejected ones. That rules out simply copying the data's length bias. Longer greedy answers loop and hit the cap, which the judge penalizes, and the repetition-penalty run supports this: with loops suppressed, DPO-UF ties SFT. A tie is not a gain, so I read the result as decoding sensitivity on top of a null effect, not as a content improvement hidden by loops. Table~\ref{tab:examples} shows that extra length can carry invented facts, but I did not measure how often. Insufficient training does not explain the loss, because the validation margin was still rising at the end of the epoch. I cannot separate over-optimization from preference-data mismatch, because I did not evaluate other checkpoints or other $\beta$ values for the DPO runs. A strong baseline is not the explanation either: 123 of SFT's 300 AlpacaEval answers contain a repeated phrase.

\paragraph{LoRA persona.} \emph{Measured.} The selected adapter produced no style the judge could see, although the same rubric picked the dataset's persona answers in all 22 control pairs. The adapter with the strongest style wrote much shorter answers (median 47 tokens against 307 for the base on the required prompts), ended on their own less often (33 against 37 of 40, and 15 against 22 of 22 on the held-out prompts), and lost on usefulness and safety. Only one of the six scored adapters passed my degeneration filter, and the last row of Table~\ref{tab:examples} shows the pattern: the strongest adapter repeats itself, and the judge prefers the base answer on safety.

\emph{Interpretation.} With 198 training pairs, I found no setting that gained style without losing coherence. Pushing harder (ten epochs at $5\times10^{-5}$, or six at $1\times10^{-4}$) raised the style score but lowered the EOS rate to 11 and 33 of 40 against 37 for the base (Table~\ref{tab:lorasweep}). The style gain therefore arrives together with the damage, so I conclude that LoRA-DPO as I ran it did not produce a usable persona.
